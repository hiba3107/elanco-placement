    Learn more about getting a tick identified

    Unfed ticks range from under 1 mm to about 4 mm. The UK Health Security Agency's Tick Surveillance Scheme identifies ticks sent in by the public. It does not test them for disease. Keep the tick in a sealed container somewhere cool until you post it.

    Preventing tick bites

    Simple habits make bites much less likely, and removing a tick quickly lowers the risk of infection.

    Before you go out

    • Wear long sleeves, and trousers tucked into your socks. Light colours make ticks easier to spot.
    • Use an insect repellent that works against ticks, such as one with DEET.
    • Keep to clear paths and avoid brushing through long grass and bracken.

    When you get back

    • Check your skin and your children's, especially armpits, groin, waistband, behind the knees, along the hairline and behind the ears.
    • Brush off your clothes before going indoors, and shower soon after.
    • Check your pets and their bedding.

    Removing a tick

    1. Use fine-tipped tweezers or a tick removal tool (pharmacies, vets and pet shops sell them).
    2. Grip the tick as close to the skin as you can and pull upwards slowly and firmly. Do not twist, squeeze or crush it.
    3. Clean the bite with antiseptic, or soap and water.
    4. Do not burn the tick or cover it in oil, alcohol or petroleum jelly. These can make things worse.

    Protecting your pets

    • Check dogs and cats after walks: head, ears, neck, armpits and between the toes.
    • Remove ticks promptly with a tick hook, twisting gently as the tool directs.
    • Ask your vet which tick treatment suits your pet and how often to use it.
    • Dogs that explore badger setts or fox earths can pick up fox/badger ticks, so check them carefully.

    When to get help

    • Contact your GP or call NHS 111 if a circular red rash spreads around the bite, or if you get flu-like symptoms. The rash can appear up to 3 months later.
    • Not everyone with Lyme disease gets a rash, so mention any recent tick bite.
    • Contact your vet if your pet seems unwell after a tick bite.

    Sources

    This is general guidance, not medical advice. It is based on the NHS and the UK Health Security Agency's tick awareness guidance.

    What happens to your report

    It is saved straight away and shown in that city's panel as an unverified community report.

    Reports do not change a city's severity, which uses Elanco's data only, so a handful of reports cannot make an area look riskier than it is.

    Taking a useful photo

    Use good light and a plain background, with a coin or ruler beside the tick for scale.

    Your privacy

    We do not ask for your name or contact details. "Use my location" only picks the nearest city in your browser; your location is never sent.

    Data quality

    Good to know

    • Elanco's records name a city but not an exact place, so each marker sits at the city centre rather than where the tick was found.
    • Recency is measured from the newest record in the data, not from today, because the data ends in December 2024. Measured from today, every city would look inactive.
    • The passerine tick. Elanco's data calls this Dermacentor frontalis. The UK government's tick species list (UKHSA) names the passerine tick Ixodes frontalis. The API keeps Elanco's value so the data matches theirs, and the species guide shows both names.
    • Every species appears in every city and sightings are spread evenly through the year, unlike real UK tick records, so treat this as sample data rather than a real risk map.
